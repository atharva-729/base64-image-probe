The question

Can a language model read a picture it can’t see?

An image file is only bytes, and bytes can be typed out as text. We pasted that text into four models and asked: what is the picture?

Short answer: yesThe strongest model rebuilt every pixel of every image.
ButOnly with heavy reasoning. It is slow and costly next to attaching the image.

  • Images7 tiny black-and-white pictures, 8 pixels by 8: four shapes and three blocks of random noise.

The full story

Ten steps from a hunch to a result

We turned tiny images into strings of letters and digits, pasted them into four language models as plain text, and asked what the picture was. No tools, no code, no vision. This is the story of what happened, including the parts where nothing did.

1 The hunch

A picture is a file, and a file can be written as text

When you attach an image to a chat, the model looks at it with a separate vision system. But an image file is only a sequence of bytes, and any bytes can be spelled out in ordinary characters. That spelling is called base64, and it looks like gibberish.

Models read base64 text surprisingly well. So the hunch was: could a model read an image that way too, working backwards from the gibberish to the picture by reasoning alone? It is like being handed a page of Morse code and asked what the drawing is.

See one picture stored five ways, exactly as the models received it ↑

2 The first try, and the objection

It seemed to work. That was the problem.

The first round was done by hand, pasting the PNG of the 7 into chat apps. Two of five answers were pixel-perfect.

Then my manager pointed out the hole in it:

A chatbot is an agent. It has access to code. It can save that string, convert it to an image, use its vision, and read it. You do not want that.

He was right. A correct answer from a chat app proves nothing, because the app may have quietly run a decoder. Everything had to be redone where cheating is impossible.

3 Take the tools away

Text in, text out, nothing else

Every run from here on goes through the API with no tools attached. The model receives characters and returns characters. Whatever it gets right, it worked out in its own reasoning.

5 The switch

Reasoning is the whole difference

GPT-6 Luna is the cheapest model here, so it was the one to sweep across reasoning settings: four images, five formats, reasoning off, low and high.

With reasoning off it scored 0 of 16 on the encoded images, with pixel accuracy of 38–67%. That is what flipping a coin gives. Turn reasoning up and the same model, on the same files, starts producing exact decodes.

6 The wall

Then the cheap model ran out of room

On PNG, GPT-6 Luna did something new. It did not answer wrongly. It did not answer at all. It reasoned until it hit its 12,000-token limit and returned an empty reply, 15 times out of 16.

So the limit was doubled to 24,000 and every failed call was sent again. That rescued some of them: 6 of the 15 came back pixel-perfect. Eight ran out of room a second time.

When Luna did finish a PNG, it was usually right: 8 of the 10 grids it produced were pixel-perfect. Its main failure was running out of room, not getting it wrong.

An empty reply is billed like any other. This was the most expensive lesson of the project.

Why some PNGs were easy

A PNG can pack its pixels in different ways. Luna breezed through both PNGs that store their pixels uncompressed, even at four times the size, and struggled with the ones that use real compression.

9 One more twist

Decoding is not recognising

Getting every pixel right and knowing what the pixels show turned out to be two different skills.

  • GPT-6 Luna decoded the X exactly, then called it “a white diamond on a black background”.
  • It decoded the 7 exactly and described “a short horizontal bar with a zigzagging stem”.
  • GPT-6 Sol decoded all seven PNGs, but four of them with black and white swapped. PNG stores black as 0 and our prompt asked for black as 1, so that trap was partly ours.
10 What it means, and what it doesn’t

Yes, they can. No, you shouldn’t.

A strong model with reasoning switched on can take the raw bytes of a small image, decompress them in its reasoning, and reproduce every pixel. That is a real capability, and it is not vision.

This is not a better way to send images. Attaching an image normally is far cheaper and far more reliable. What this measures is how far reasoning alone can go on raw data.

Read with care

  • Small counts. Seven images, one run per cell. Enough to see the pattern, not enough for fine comparisons.
  • Tiny images. These are 20–130 characters of base64. A real photo is hundreds of thousands. Nothing here says the result carries over.
  • Limits cut both ways. A model that hit its token limit did not finish; that is not the same as being unable.
  • Unequal settings. Each model has a different range of reasoning effort, so each is shown where it reasons.
